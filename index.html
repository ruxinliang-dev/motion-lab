About

I am Ruxin Liang. My work is in 3D; I explore interface design independently. This lab collects the visual and interaction questions I am working through.

I start with a formula, choose its parameters and adjust them until the result feels right. I build with Claude's help; choosing the direction and refining the behaviour are my design work.

The artwork is generated from formulas, the KeePlan prototype runs in the browser, and its icons are SVG paths. Experiment 03 also powers the expanding detail panels. The motion control follows your system preference and lets you pause animation.

The flow

Pick a step, or use Next. The screens are live: check a topic, create a task, join an event, hide a profile field.

The design question

Needfinding began with observation, and two words: plan and task.

The project began with young people who wanted to explore new interests but felt held back by family expectations and employment pressure. What makes taking the first step feel difficult?

I interviewed six people using open-ended questions about plans and tasks. I recorded their responses and organised the answers in Google Forms to understand their needs before proposing a solution.

The conflict point

Interviewees valued planning but struggled to begin. Fear of leaving a task unfinished could make them feel frustrated before they started.

The problem

Participants described pressure from family, friends and their wider environment. These pressures accumulated and made it harder to feel motivated.

Some connected this loss of confidence to repeated setbacks. They wanted a way to regain confidence and try again.

Empathy map · what they think

The load is already inside

  • Confused about what to do when they wake up every day
  • Too much content in their head, and no way to arrange it
  • One thing left unfinished drains the motivation for everything else
  • Anxious about the future of their life
Empathy map · what they see and hear

And the comparison is constant

  • The people around them know when to do things
  • A supervisor or professor asking for a task as soon as possible
  • Friends posting travel photos, with hobbies and full days
  • Family encouraging them to improve their living conditions
Empathy map · analysis

What that adds up to

The empathy map linked pressure to high expectations, comparison and little room for mistakes. Seeing other people appear successful could make participants feel further behind.

This suggested a direction: help people explore an interest beyond the immediate setback, at a pace they can choose.

What the research found

Two follow-up rounds used Google Forms and SurveyMonkey. Six people took part in the initial interviews; the later rounds also included new participants.

Sources of stress

Five named causes

  • Peer competitiveness
  • Employment pressure
  • Financial situation
  • Satisfaction with results
  • Family pressure

Participants described work as more uncertain than study. Alongside practical concerns, their own and other people's expectations added pressure.

De-stressing

How participants take a break

Participants most often described talking with close family and friends, followed by activities they enjoyed, such as music, games and sport.

Travel and counselling felt less accessible in their routines. They described fresh experiences, from a new place to a gift or fresh air, as refreshing.

Visual tendency

What they want to look at

Participants preferred sharing interests over personal details. An avatar and a few interests felt sufficient to begin a conversation.

They wanted simple interfaces that took little effort to learn, with a bright environment and a preference for light green and white.

The decision the project turned on

Point of view, and the solution I did not build.

The research informed a scenario: a 26-year-old between jobs after an internship, facing financial pressure and family expectations.

A job-finding app was one possible response. I chose to explore a broader need raised in the interviews: finding space to recover and try something new.

Finding work might address an immediate concern, but the interviews also pointed to emotional pressure and uncertainty. A job alone would not necessarily resolve the other pressures in this scenario.

I focused on everyday opportunities to explore interests and connect with others. This gave the project a manageable scope without promising to solve every source of pressure.

The question became: how might we help people regain their interest in daily life? KeePlan brings task lists and reminders together with interest-based events. Local discovery by zip code remains part of the proposed concept.

Design goals

Relieve stress and plan. Seven goals, straight out of the brief.

Provide a calendar with reminders for events
Keys

How privacy is meant to work

Users are authenticated when they register, and after that everything another user can see is under their control. Apart from an avatar and a personal description, every field can be hidden. The design intent is that joining an event does not automatically reveal additional personal details; the live demo illustrates the visibility controls.

People who want to try socialising can meet others with the same hobbies through events. People who do not want the added pressure can still take new information from the feed. The platform builds social relationships indirectly and gives people the right to choose.

Design priorities · Maslow

Where it sits

Self-actualization. To provide a place where people can express their creativity, satisfy their desire to explore new things, and release their accumulated stress.

Love and belonging. To help people make friends with others who share the same preferences, gently, on a social platform.

Target group

Who it is for

Main audience. Young people, students, people between jobs, workers and travellers. These are the people the program is for and they have the largest effect on it.

Secondary. Parents, families, fitness people. They affect the project, less so.

Design decisions

Make the first step feel possible, then let people choose how far to go.

The interviews exposed a contradiction: people valued planning, yet feared failing at the plans they made. The affinity work also connected that pressure to comparison, isolation and repetitive routines. I chose to bring small plans, shared interests and control over personal information into one product. Each serves a different part of the same starting problem.

01 · Interviews & empathy map

Keep the next action close

Put one manageable next step within reach.

Design response. The Home screen puts current tasks beside reminders, with a persistent add action. Calendar and Reminder provide another way back to a plan, so remembering it does not depend on holding it in mind.

Research & trade-offs

What I heard. People wanted a quick way to record things, while an unfinished task could drain their motivation for everything else.

Trade-off. A planner can become another source of obligation. The next test should check whether someone can add one manageable task without feeling they must organise their whole week.

02 · De-stressing & affinity analysis

Let an interest start the conversation

Start with a shared activity, then let a conversation follow.

Design response. Events centres the activity before the person. Someone can browse, express interest or join; messages then provide a place to coordinate with another person.

Research & trade-offs

What I heard. Participants described familiar people, enjoyable activities and fresh experiences as ways they took a break from pressure.

Trade-off. More invitations can add social pressure. Activity intensity labels are a design hypothesis for making the choice easier on a low-energy day; their usefulness still needs testing.

03 · Privacy preferences & design goals

Make disclosure a choice

Let people participate without disclosing every personal detail.

Design response. Edit Profile gives each personal field a visibility control. Joining an activity and deciding what to disclose are separate choices in the proposed experience.

Research & trade-offs

What I heard. People preferred sharing interests to exposing personal details. The research treated privacy as part of feeling comfortable enough to participate.

Trade-off. A visibility icon can be misunderstood. The prototype demonstrates the controls; a usability test should ask people to explain what another participant could see. Server-side privacy enforcement is outside this interface demo.

04 · Empathy map & statistics concept

Show progress without a streak to protect

Make progress readable without introducing a streak or ranking.

Design response. Statistics pairs a completion arc with explicit To do, Done and Left counts. It gives a readable summary without adding a streak or ranking against other people.

Research & trade-offs

What I heard. Comparison and high expectations were recurring pressures, while completing something could feel encouraging.

Trade-off. Even a count of unfinished work can feel discouraging. Whether this presentation encourages a return after a missed day remains an open question.

What the feedback supports

Encouraging responses, with specific reasons to keep iterating.

Prototype questionnaire · 10 respondents

The idea was understood after an explanation

Nine of ten respondents said they understood the problem KeePlan addressed after listening to the designer's explanation; one was unsure. Seven chose the highest app rating and three chose the next highest.

This supports interest in the concept. It does not establish that people can discover the flow unaided, complete tasks successfully or experience less stress in everyday use.

Reported improvements · multiple selections allowed

Clarity needs a closer look

Three respondents flagged unclear type, one flagged visual colour and one flagged navigation. These counts describe overlapping responses, rather than separate groups.

The interface response prioritises labelled navigation, a consistent type hierarchy and clear state feedback. A follow-up test should cover creating a task, joining an event and hiding a profile field without guidance, recording errors and participants' explanations.

Clarity before decoration

A light surface and a restrained set of signals.

The research favoured a bright environment and described a preference for light green and white. I kept the light surface and chose a blue-to-cyan accent for this interface. The accent is my design choice, not a colour preference established by the survey.

Colour highlights actions and state; labels and numbers carry the meaning. Consistent icons support recognition across planning and events. The palette and typography still need evaluation in the complete interface, particularly given the feedback about legibility.

The interface uses a shared set of SVG icons. Reusing their shapes across screens lets the same symbol keep its meaning when its colour or context changes.

Make state changes understandable

Feedback where the action happens.

The create button moves through waiting and confirmation in the same place, so the person can follow the result of their action. A moving segment marker connects a selection to its content; brief screen transitions preserve a sense of direction.

These are interaction choices to evaluate, rather than measured usability improvements. The reduced-motion option removes transitional movement while keeping the resulting state visible.
